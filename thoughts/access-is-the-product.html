Access Is the Product

August 28, 2026  ·  3 min read

A restaurant table is one of the strangest forms of inventory. At 7:30 it can be worth hundreds of dollars. At 7:31, if nobody sits down, it is worth nothing.

That makes dining look like a straightforward marketplace problem. Find unused supply, find people who want it, and match the two more efficiently. More restaurants, more diners, more reservations. Liquidity wins.

Building Aside has made me think the opposite is true at the premium end of the market. The difficult part is not helping someone book any table. It is helping them trust that this is the right table.

Premium experiences work because somebody made a decision before the customer arrived. This restaurant belongs. This room feels right. This person will enjoy being here. The value is not just availability; it is judgment compressed into access. Remove the judgment and the product becomes another list.

This creates an unusual marketplace. A traditional marketplace wants maximum supply. A premium marketplace needs coherent supply. A traditional marketplace treats every additional listing as progress. A premium marketplace sometimes gets better by saying no. The constraint is not inventory. It is taste.

The same is true on the demand side. Restaurants do not want abstract traffic. They want guests who fit the room, respect the experience, return, and bring the right people with them. Diners do not want a database of availability. They want the confidence that choosing from a smaller set will lead to a better night.

That is why the brand cannot sit on top of the marketplace like decoration. The brand is the mechanism that makes both sides trust the selection. It tells the diner that the venue was chosen deliberately, and it tells the venue that the audience was, too. In a premium marketplace, curation is not a marketing strategy. It is the operating system.

The more I work on Aside, the more I see how much of the product exists outside the app. It lives in restaurant conversations, onboarding, the tone of the brand, the rules around access, and the small decisions that determine whether the experience feels considered or merely scarce. Software coordinates the system. It does not create the taste.

The best premium products do not sell abundance. They remove the work of choosing from abundance. They make a point of view useful. In that kind of marketplace, access is not a feature layered onto the product. Access is the product.
